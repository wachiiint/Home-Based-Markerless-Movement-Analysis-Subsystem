# 05 — User Manual

> ⚠️ **Draft — source content, not the final deliverable.**
> The manual patients and caretakers actually receive will be a **designed 1 to 3 page handout**
> (PDF, printed sheet, or images) with diagrams of each pose, not this text document. This file is
> the written source that handout is built from: the wording, the step order, the muscle information,
> and the reference values. Treat it as the script, not the artwork.
> Illustrations still needed: the camera setup diagram, and one figure per pose showing the starting
> position and the direction of movement.

> For the person recording and reading the assessment: a clinician, a caretaker, or the patient.
> No technical knowledge assumed. Terms are defined in [00-glossary.md](00-glossary.md).

---

## Safety first

> **Stop immediately if the movement causes pain.** These tests measure how far a joint moves
> comfortably, not how far it can be forced.
>
> - Keep a sturdy chair or wall within reach for balance.
> - Someone should be nearby for any standing test if the patient is unsteady.
> - The patient sets the pace. Slow is better than fast, and a smaller comfortable movement is better
>   than a larger forced one.
> - Never push, pull, or assist the limb. The system measures what the patient can do alone.

# Part 1 — What you need

| Item | Notes |
|------|-------|
| A smartphone | Any modern phone. Higher resolution helps but is not required |
| Something to hold the phone | A small tripod, a stack of books, a shelf. **Not** a hand — shaking spoils the result |
| Space | About 2 to 3 metres between the phone and the patient, and room to move freely |
| A firm chair | Without arms if possible. Needed for the seated tests |
| Suitable clothing | Shorts or fitted trousers. Loose or long trousers hide the knee and ankle |
| Bare feet or thin shoes | Bulky shoes hide the toes, which ankle tests need |

---

# Part 2 — Setting up

Do this once per recording session. Good setup matters more than anything else — most failed
recordings are setup problems, not software problems.

### 1. Place the phone

- **Height:** about the patient's hip height.
- **Angle:** level, not tilted up or down.
- **Direction:** filming the patient **from the side**, not the front. You should see the patient's
  profile — one shoulder, one hip, one knee.
- **Distance:** far enough that the **whole body** stays in frame throughout the movement.

```
        (side view)

   patient                  phone
     |  ○                    ▭  ← at hip height, level
     | /|\                   |
     |/ | \                  |
       / \        ←  2–3 m  →
      /   \
```

### 2. Check the background

Plain and uncluttered. **Nobody else should be visible.** The system picks the clearest person it
finds, and a bystander standing still in good light can be chosen instead of the patient.

### 3. Check the lighting

Even and bright. Avoid a window or lamp **behind** the patient — that turns them into a dark
silhouette the system cannot read.

### 4. Do a test recording

Record five seconds, play it back, and confirm the whole body stays in frame and the limbs are
clearly visible. This takes ten seconds and saves a wasted session.

# Part 3 — The six movements

Each section below gives the objective, the muscles involved, and exactly how to perform it.

**How to read the muscle information.** Every movement has muscles that **shorten** to create the
motion, and opposing muscles that **stretch** to allow it. The 3D motion simulation colours five
muscle groups per leg, so you can watch this happen. Remember that the overlay shows muscle *length*,
not effort or strength — a camera cannot measure force.

**About the reference values.** The expected and borderline figures are the system's reference points
for *this test*, not textbook full joint range. They are set by convention and have not yet been
validated against a clinical population — see
[07-evaluation-and-limitations.md](07-evaluation-and-limitations.md).

---

## 3.1 Knee flexion

**Objective:** measure how far the knee **bends** — knee flexion ROM.

**Why it matters:** bending the knee is needed to sit down, climb stairs, walk normally, and get into
a car. Restricted knee flexion shows up early in the loss of independent movement.

| Muscles | |
|---------|--|
| **Shortening (doing the work)** | Hamstrings — biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus, at the back of the thigh. The calf muscle assists |
| **Stretching (allowing it)** | Quadriceps, at the front of the thigh |
| **Watch in the overlay** | Hamstrings shorten, quadriceps lengthen |

**Position:** sitting on the chair, back straight, both feet flat on the floor, thighs supported.

**How to perform:**
1. Start with the foot flat on the floor.
2. Slide the foot backward under the chair, bending the knee as far as is comfortable.
3. Hold for one second.
4. Return the foot to the start.
5. Repeat 3 to 5 times, slowly.

**Camera:** side view, filming the side of the leg being tested.

**Reference:** expected 60 degrees or more · borderline below 40 degrees.

**Common mistakes:** lifting the whole thigh instead of sliding the foot back; leaning the body
sideways; the chair hiding the lower leg from the camera — sit near the front edge.

---

## 3.2 Knee extension

**Objective:** measure how far the knee **straightens** — knee extension ROM.

**Why it matters:** straightening the knee against gravity is what lifts the body out of a chair and
up a step. The quadriceps is the muscle most affected by sarcopenia, so this test is the closest of
the six to the condition being screened for.

| Muscles | |
|---------|--|
| **Shortening (doing the work)** | Quadriceps femoris — rectus femoris and the three vastus muscles, at the front of the thigh |
| **Stretching (allowing it)** | Hamstrings, at the back of the thigh |
| **Watch in the overlay** | Quadriceps shorten, hamstrings lengthen |

**Position:** sitting on the chair, back straight, both feet flat on the floor.

**How to perform:**
1. Start with the foot flat on the floor.
2. Straighten the knee, lifting the foot forward until the leg is as straight as is comfortable.
3. Hold for one second.
4. Lower slowly — do not let the leg drop.
5. Repeat 3 to 5 times.

**Camera:** side view, filming the side of the leg being tested.

**Reference:** expected 20 degrees or more · borderline below 10 degrees.

**Common mistakes:** dropping the leg instead of lowering it, which spoils the smoothness reading;
leaning back to help the lift; swinging for momentum instead of lifting steadily.

---

## 3.3 Hip flexion

**Objective:** measure how far the thigh lifts **forward and up** — hip flexion ROM.

**Why it matters:** lifting the thigh is how a person climbs stairs, steps over an obstacle, gets
into a car, and puts on shoes. Reduced hip flexion is a common cause of tripping.

| Muscles | |
|---------|--|
| **Shortening (doing the work)** | Iliopsoas — the deep hip flexor running from the lower spine to the thigh. Rectus femoris assists |
| **Stretching (allowing it)** | Gluteals (buttock muscles) and hamstrings |
| **Watch in the overlay** | Iliopsoas shortens, gluteals lengthen |

**Position:** standing upright, side-on to the camera, one hand resting on a chair back or wall for
balance.

**How to perform:**
1. Stand tall, weight on the leg that is not being tested.
2. Lift the tested knee forward and upward as far as is comfortable, as if marching slowly.
3. Hold for one second.
4. Lower the foot to the floor with control.
5. Repeat 3 to 5 times.

**Camera:** side view, capturing the full body from head to feet.

**Reference:** expected 40 degrees or more · borderline below 25 degrees.

**Common mistakes:** leaning backward to raise the knee higher — keep the back straight; gripping the
chair hard enough to pull the body up; rushing so the movement becomes a swing.

---

## 3.4 Hip extension

**Objective:** measure how far the thigh moves **backward** — hip extension ROM.

**Why it matters:** the backward push of the leg is what propels a person forward when walking. Weak
hip extension shortens the stride and slows gait speed, one of the main screening measures for
sarcopenia.

| Muscles | |
|---------|--|
| **Shortening (doing the work)** | Gluteus maximus, the large buttock muscle, with the hamstrings assisting |
| **Stretching (allowing it)** | Iliopsoas and rectus femoris, at the front of the hip and thigh |
| **Watch in the overlay** | Gluteals shorten, iliopsoas lengthens |

**Position:** standing upright, side-on to the camera, both hands resting lightly on a chair back.

**How to perform:**
1. Stand tall with the back straight.
2. Move the tested leg backward, keeping the knee straight, as far as is comfortable.
3. Hold for one second.
4. Return the foot to the floor.
5. Repeat 3 to 5 times.

**Camera:** side view. This movement is small, so it is important that the camera is level and
directly to the side.

**Reference:** expected 20 degrees or more · borderline below 10 degrees.

**Common mistakes:** arching the lower back to fake extra range — this is the most common error in
this test; leaning the trunk forward as the leg goes back; bending the knee.

---

## 3.5 Ankle dorsiflexion

**Objective:** measure how far the foot lifts **upward** at the ankle, toes toward the shin — ankle
dorsiflexion ROM.

**Why it matters:** this is what lifts the toes clear of the ground during each step. Reduced
dorsiflexion is a direct and well-established cause of tripping and falling, and it also makes
walking down stairs and slopes difficult.

| Muscles | |
|---------|--|
| **Shortening (doing the work)** | Tibialis anterior, the muscle along the front of the shin, with the toe extensors |
| **Stretching (allowing it)** | Calf muscles — gastrocnemius and soleus |
| **Watch in the overlay** | Gastrocnemius lengthens |

**Position:** sitting on the chair with the tested leg extended forward, heel resting on the floor.

**How to perform:**
1. Rest the heel on the floor with the leg straight.
2. Pull the toes and foot upward toward the shin, as far as is comfortable.
3. Hold for one second.
4. Relax the foot back down.
5. Repeat 3 to 5 times.

**Camera:** side view, close enough that the **foot and toes fill a good part of the frame** while
the leg remains visible.

**Reference:** expected 15 degrees or more · borderline below 8 degrees.

**Common mistakes:** wearing shoes — bare feet or thin socks are much better; lifting the whole leg
instead of only the foot; sitting too far from the camera, so the small movement is lost.

> **Note:** ankle measurements are the least reliable of the six, because the toes are small in the
> frame and easily hidden. Take extra care with camera distance and footwear here.

---

## 3.6 Ankle plantarflexion

**Objective:** measure how far the foot points **downward** at the ankle — ankle plantarflexion ROM.

**Why it matters:** this is the push-off that drives the body forward in every step. The calf muscle
producing it is measured directly in sarcopenia screening through calf circumference, so this test
looks at the function of a muscle the clinical guidelines already care about.

| Muscles | |
|---------|--|
| **Shortening (doing the work)** | Gastrocnemius and soleus, the calf muscles |
| **Stretching (allowing it)** | Tibialis anterior, along the front of the shin |
| **Watch in the overlay** | Gastrocnemius shortens |

**Position:** sitting on the chair with the tested leg extended forward, heel resting on the floor.

**How to perform:**
1. Rest the heel on the floor with the leg straight.
2. Point the foot and toes away from the body, as far as is comfortable.
3. Hold for one second.
4. Relax the foot back.
5. Repeat 3 to 5 times.

**Camera:** side view, with the foot clearly visible.

**Reference:** expected 25 degrees or more · borderline below 15 degrees.

**Common mistakes:** lifting the heel off the floor and rotating the whole leg; bending the knee to
create the appearance of more movement; bulky shoes hiding the toes.

# Part 4 — Recording and submitting

## Recording

1. Start the video.
2. The patient **stands or sits still for 2 to 3 seconds** before moving. This gives the system a
   personal starting reference.
3. Perform the movement **slowly**, 3 to 5 times.
4. Stop the video. Ten to fifteen seconds is plenty — longer recordings do not improve the result.

## Submitting

Provide, along with the video:

| Field | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| Patient ID | The identifier for this patient, e.g. `PT-001`. Must be the same every visit |
| Task | Which of the six movements was performed |
| **Side** | **Which leg moved — left or right.** Get this right; symmetry depends on it |
| View | Lateral, meaning filmed from the side. This is the normal choice |

---

# Part 5 — Reading the result

Read in this order. The first item can invalidate everything after it.

### 1. Was the recording accepted?

If the result says **rejected**, the video was not good enough to measure and no numbers are given.
This is deliberate — a wrong number is worse than no number. The reason is stated. Fix the setup
using Part 2 and record again.

### 2. Watch the annotated video

The skeleton is drawn over the patient. **Check that it actually follows the patient's body.** If it
jumps to another person, or drifts off the limbs, the numbers describe something other than the
patient, no matter how confident they look. This is the single most useful check available.

### 3. Check the analysis mode

Either **3D** or **2D**. Both are valid. 2D is more sensitive to camera placement, so a 2D result
depends more heavily on having filmed squarely from the side.

### 4. Read the numbers

| Number | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **ROM** | How far the joint moved, in degrees. The main result |
| **Maximum / minimum angle** | The extremes of the movement |
| **Peak velocity** | How fast the joint moved. Low velocity with normal ROM suggests weakness or caution rather than stiffness |
| **Smoothness** | How controlled the movement was. Jerky, hesitant motion scores lower |
| **Confidence** | How much the recording itself can be trusted, from 0 to 1 |

### 5. Read the graph

The graph shows the angle through time — the shape of the movement, which a single ROM number hides.

- **Smooth repeated arcs:** a good, controlled movement.
- **Jagged or stepped:** hesitancy, tremor, or difficulty.
- **Flattening at the top:** the patient reached a limit and stopped.
- **Each repetition smaller than the last:** fatigue, which is itself a clinical finding.

Both legs are drawn on one time axis: the instructed leg as a solid line, the other as a faint dashed
one for comparison. Hovering reads out the angle of each leg at that moment. A **break in a line**
means the leg was not clearly visible in those frames — nothing is drawn across a gap, because that
part of the movement was never measured. The same series can be downloaded as a CSV, one row per
sampled frame, from the export row below the graph.

### 6. Understand the risk level

| Level | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| **Low** | ROM at or above the expected reference, quality good |
| **Moderate** | ROM below expected but above borderline |
| **High** | ROM below borderline, or the recording quality was poor |

> This is a **screening indicator, not a diagnosis.** It says a movement looked restricted in one
> recording. A clinician decides what that means.

### 7. Watch the motion simulation

A 3D replay of the movement that can be rotated and scrubbed. Turning on the muscle overlay shows the
five muscle groups stretching and shortening, coloured by length. Use it to see the movement pattern
and to explain the movement to a patient — but remember it shows muscle *length*, never force or
effort.

# Part 6 — Comparing left with right, and tracking progress

Both comparisons need **two recordings**, so they are requested separately after recording.

**Left against right (symmetry):**
1. Record the task on one leg.
2. Record the **same task** on the other leg, same day, same setup.
3. Ask for the symmetry result.

A difference above about 10 percent is worth a clinician's attention. The system refuses to answer if
the two recordings are not properly comparable, rather than giving a misleading number.

**Progress over time:**
1. Record the task today.
2. Repeat at the next visit, using the **same task, same leg, and same setup**.
3. Ask for the progress result.

The system reports whether the change is **clinically meaningful** or just measurement noise. For
knee ROM, an improvement of about 5 degrees is the point where the change becomes real.

> Keeping the setup identical between visits matters more than anything else here. A different camera
> angle can change the measurement by more than genuine improvement would.

# Part 7 — Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| Recording rejected, low valid frames | Patient partly out of frame, or too far away | Move the phone back; check the whole body stays in view during the movement |
| Recording rejected, low confidence | Poor lighting, or loose clothing hiding joints | Brighten the room; wear shorts or fitted trousers |
| Skeleton followed the wrong person | Someone else in frame | Clear the background completely |
| Skeleton jumps or flickers | Handheld phone, or a busy background | Rest the phone on something solid; use a plain wall behind |
| Result came back 2D, not 3D | The 3D reconstruction failed its own check | Normal and acceptable. Improve the side-on angle and stability to raise the chance of 3D |
| ROM looks much lower than expected | Camera not truly side-on, foreshortening the movement | Reposition squarely to the side at hip height |
| Ankle result looks wrong | Shoes, or the foot too small in frame | Bare feet; move the phone closer while keeping the leg visible |
| Symmetry unavailable | Only one leg has been recorded | Record the same task on the other leg |
| Symmetry says not comparable | Different task, view, or too long between the recordings | Record both legs in the same session with the same setup |
